CHAPTER 02 / THE GEOGRAPHY

A geography of vulnerability

Threatened wildlife occurs across Australia's forests, wetlands, deserts and coastlines. These maps explore how threatened-taxon listings, observations and conservation areas vary across the country.

FIGURE 04 / AREA-NORMALISED GEOGRAPHY

Where are threatened taxa most concentrated relative to land area?

This map compares the number of nationally listed threatened animal taxa per 100,000 km² of state or territory land area. Darker colours represent higher area-normalised values.

Hover over each state to see its original count, land area and normalised value. The colour legend uses a logarithmic scale because the ACT is much smaller than the other jurisdictions.

What stands out: Tasmania has approximately 163.7 listed threatened taxa per 100,000 km², higher than many mainland states. The ACT has an especially large ratio because its land area is very small. These figures do not directly measure animal abundance or conservation risk.

Sources: EPBC Threatened Species Lists (2026), Geoscience Australia land-area figures, and ABS state boundaries (2021). Taxa can occur in multiple states.

FIGURE 05 / BIRD OBSERVATION HOTSPOTS

Where have Australia's threatened birds been observed?

This map shows recorded observations of three threatened Australian bird species between 2015 and 2025. Each hexagon covers a similar geographic area. Darker hexagons represent more recorded observations.

Choose a bird species using the dropdown. Hover over a hexagon to see the number of observation records. Hexagons are approximately 50 km across from corner to corner.

What stands out: Observation hotspots vary between Swift Parrot, Regent Honeyeater and Orange-bellied Parrot. More observations do not necessarily mean more individual birds: survey effort and repeated sightings influence the results.

Source: Australian biodiversity occurrence records, 2015–2025, filtered to include georeferenced observations. The map shows recorded observations, not estimated population density.

FIGURE 06 / PROTECTED AREAS

How much of Australia's land is protected?

Protected areas play an important role in conserving Australia's biodiversity. This map compares the total protected land area across Australia's states and territories using CAPAD 2024 data. Larger circles represent more protected land, while darker circles show a higher percentage of land protected within each jurisdiction.

Hover over a circle to see protected land in square kilometres and the percentage of land protected. Each circle represents the whole state or territory, not individual protected-area locations.

What stands out: Western Australia has the largest total protected land area, at approximately 786,742 km². However, Tasmania has a greater proportion of land protected, at 42.8%, compared with Western Australia's 31.1%.

Source: Australian Government, CAPAD 2024 . Terrestrial protected-area statistics by jurisdiction. Map boundaries: Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2021.

FIGURE 07 / THE BUSHFIRE FOOTPRINT

Where were larger fire footprints mapped in southeastern Australia?

The 2019–20 bushfire season placed enormous pressure on Australian ecosystems. This map highlights selected larger mapped fire footprints in New South Wales and Victoria during the 2019–20 financial year. Orange areas represent simplified fire boundaries, not fire severity.

Use the dropdown to view both states or select NSW or Victoria individually. Hover over the orange areas for details.

What stands out: Many larger mapped fire footprints occur along the eastern and southeastern regions of NSW and Victoria. These landscapes support important wildlife habitats, making bushfire exposure a concern for biodiversity conservation.

CHAPTER 03 / THE THREATS

What is putting wildlife at risk?

Australia's threatened animals face more than one kind of danger. Habitat changes, invasive species, fires and climate pressures affect different animal groups in different ways.

FIGURE 08 / THREATS BY ANIMAL GROUP

Which threats affect each animal group most?

This heatmap compares eight major threats across six animal groups. Each cell shows the percentage of assessed taxa within that group affected by a particular threat. Darker cells indicate higher percentages.

Hover over each cell to see the number of affected taxa and the total assessed. A taxon can face multiple threats, so the percentages do not add up to 100%.

What stands out: Invasive species and diseases affect 80.4% of assessed mammal taxa. Habitat loss is recorded for 74.5% of assessed fish taxa, while changed water regimes affect 62.7%.

Source: Ward et al. (2021) . Data reflect the study's earlier threat assessments, including EPBC status information from 2018. Only fauna were included. Percentages use the number of assessed taxa within each animal group.

CHAPTER 04 / THE FUTURE

Can conservation turn the tide?

The scale of biodiversity loss is serious, but conservation action can make a difference. Understanding population trends, protected areas and conservation targets helps show how much progress has been made and what remains to be done.

A CONTRIBUTOR TO CONSERVATION GROWTH

Indigenous Protected Areas have played an important role

According to Australian Bureau of Statistics reporting, Indigenous Protected Areas expanded from approximately 2% of Australia's landmass in 2004 to around 11% in 2024. These areas are managed by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander groups in accordance with Traditional Owners' objectives.

What stands out: Australia's terrestrial protected-area share increased from 10.52% in 2004 to 22.57% in 2024. This represents a gain of 12.05 percentage points over two decades.

Sources: Australian Government, historical Collaborative Australian Protected Areas Database (CAPAD) reporting, 2004–2024. Indigenous Protected Area context: Australian Bureau of Statistics. Reporting years are snapshots, not a continuous annual data series.

The upper chart shows the protected share and the remaining gap to 30%. The lower chart compares two average annual percentage-point increases. Hover over the bars for further detail.

The challenge: Australia had protected 22.57% of its landmass by 2024, leaving a gap of 7.43 percentage points to reach 30%. Reaching this benchmark by 2030 would require an average increase of approximately 1.24 percentage points per year from the 2024 level. That is about twice the average annual increase recorded between 2004 and 2024.

What stands out: Protected-area coverage has grown, but meeting a 30% benchmark would require faster expansion than the average pace of the previous two decades. Protecting land is important, but the ecological quality, location and management of protected areas also matter for wildlife recovery.

Sources: CAPAD historical protected-area reporting (2004–2024), as analysed in Figure 12. Calculations: 30.00 - 22.57 = 7.43 percentage points; 7.43 / 6 = approximately 1.24 percentage points per year. The comparison is illustrative: it is not a forecast of future protected-area growth.

THE STORY DOES NOT END HERE

Protecting wildlife means protecting the places and systems that sustain it.

Australia's threatened wildlife faces overlapping environmental pressures and substantial population declines. Expanding protected areas is part of the response, but effective conservation also depends on habitat quality, managing threats, long-term monitoring and supporting species recovery.

ABOUT THE DATA

This visualisation combines data from Australian EPBC threatened species lists, the Threatened Species Index, biodiversity observation records, the CAPAD protected-area database, national bushfire boundaries and Ward et al. (2021). Different charts use different reporting periods, definitions and populations. Values should not be interpreted as measurements of the same sample.

Counts of threatened taxa do not represent individual animals. Occurrence records reflect recorded sightings and can be influenced by survey effort. Threat categories can overlap. Population indices measure relative changes in monitored populations.
