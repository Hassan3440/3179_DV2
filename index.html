CHAPTER 01 / THE BIODIVERSITY CRISIS

The scale of the problem

Some animal groups have more threatened taxa than others. But the number of listings is only part of the story. Their level of risk also matters.

FIGURE 01 / CONSERVATION STATUS

Threatened animals do not all face the same risk

Wider columns represent more threatened taxa. The coloured sections show the proportion belonging to each conservation status.

Hover for exact values. Click an animal group to highlight it and double-click to reset.

What stands out: Birds have the most listed threatened taxa (164), but the severity of listings differs across groups.

Source: EPBC Threatened Species Lists, 28 August 2026. Listed taxa can include subspecies.

FIGURE 02 / POPULATION CHANGE

A long-term decline in monitored bird populations

This index tracks the relative abundance of monitored Australian bird populations between 1985 and 2022.

Hover over the points to see the population index and its lower and upper estimates.

What stands out: The bird population index fell from 100 in 1985 to approximately 44.2 in 2022, a decline of around 55.8%.

Source: Threatened Species Index (TSX 2025), Birds export. This is an index of monitored populations, not a census of all Australian birds. The sample includes near-threatened taxa.

FIGURE 03 / CRITICAL RISK

How does critical risk compare between animal groups?

Each animal group contains 100 dots. Dark purple dots show the approximate percentage of its threatened taxa classified as Critically Endangered.

Each dot represents approximately 1%. Hover to see the exact values.

What stands out: 43.4% of threatened invertebrate taxa are Critically Endangered, compared with approximately 8.0% of mammals.

Source: EPBC Threatened Species Lists, 28 August 2026. Percentages are calculated within each threatened animal group.

CHAPTER 02 / THE GEOGRAPHY

A geography of vulnerability

Threatened wildlife occurs across Australia's forests, wetlands, deserts and coastlines. These maps explore how threatened-taxon listings, observations and conservation areas vary across the country.

FIGURE 04 / AREA-NORMALISED GEOGRAPHY

Where are threatened taxa most concentrated relative to land area?

This map compares the number of nationally listed threatened animal taxa per 100,000 km² of state or territory land area. Darker colours represent higher area-normalised values.

Hover over each state to see its original count, land area and normalised value. The colour legend uses a logarithmic scale because the ACT is much smaller than the other jurisdictions.

What stands out: Tasmania has approximately 163.7 listed threatened taxa per 100,000 km², much higher than many mainland states. The ACT has an especially large ratio because its land area is very small. These figures do not directly measure animal abundance or conservation risk.

Sources: EPBC Threatened Species Lists (2026), Geoscience Australia land-area figures, and ABS state boundaries (2021). Taxa can occur in multiple states.

FIGURE 05 / BIRD OBSERVATION HOTSPOTS

Where have Australia's threatened birds been observed?

This map shows recorded observations of three threatened Australian bird species between 2015 and 2025. Each hexagon covers a similar geographic area. Darker hexagons represent more recorded observations.

Choose a bird species using the dropdown. Hover over a hexagon to see the number of observation records. Hexagons are approximately 50 km across from corner to corner.

What stands out: The Swift Parrot has more observation records in this dataset than the other two bird species. However, more observations do not necessarily mean more birds. Survey effort and repeated sightings can affect the results.

Source: GBIF occurrence records, Australia, 2015–2025. Records were filtered for presence, available coordinates and no flagged geospatial issues. GBIF download DOIs will be added before final submission.

FIGURE 06 / PROTECTED AREAS

How much of Australia's land is protected?

Protected areas play an important role in conserving Australia's biodiversity. This map compares the total protected land area across Australia's states and territories using CAPAD 2024 data. Larger circles represent more protected land, while darker circles show a higher percentage of land protected within each jurisdiction.

Hover over a circle to see the protected area in square kilometres and the percentage of land protected. Each circle represents the whole state or territory and does not show the exact location of individual protected areas.

What stands out: Western Australia has the largest total protected land area, at approximately 786,742 km². However, Tasmania has a greater proportion of its land protected, at 42.8%, compared with Western Australia's 31.1%. This shows why both total protected area and percentage protected are useful.

Source: Australian Government, CAPAD 2024 , terrestrial protected-area statistics by jurisdiction. Map boundaries: Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2021.

FIGURE 07 / THE BUSHFIRE FOOTPRINT

Where were larger fire footprints mapped in southeastern Australia?

The 2019–20 bushfire season placed enormous pressure on Australian ecosystems. This map highlights selected larger mapped fire footprints in New South Wales and Victoria during the 2019–20 financial year. Orange areas represent simplified fire boundaries, not fire severity.

Use the dropdown to view both states or select NSW or Victoria individually. Hover over the orange areas for details.

What stands out: Many larger mapped fire footprints occur along the eastern and southeastern regions of NSW and Victoria. These landscapes support important wildlife habitats, making bushfire exposure a concern for biodiversity conservation.

Source: Australian Government, 2019–20 Financial Year Bushfire Boundaries . Selected NSW and Victoria polygons only. Smaller source polygons were excluded, and the remaining coverage was merged and approximated using a 3 km grid. The map does not represent every fire or the severity of burning.

CHAPTER 03 / THE THREATS

What is putting wildlife at risk?

Australia's threatened animals face more than one kind of danger. Habitat changes, invasive species, fires and climate pressures affect different animal groups in different ways.

FIGURE 08 / THREATS BY ANIMAL GROUP

Which threats affect each animal group most?

This heatmap compares eight major threats across six animal groups. Each cell shows the percentage of assessed taxa within that group affected by a particular threat. Darker cells indicate higher percentages.

Hover over each cell to see the number of affected taxa and the total assessed. A taxon can face multiple threats, so the percentages do not add up to 100%.

What stands out: Invasive species and diseases affect 80.4% of the assessed mammal taxa. Habitat loss is recorded for 74.5% of assessed fish taxa, while changed water regimes affect 62.7%. The pattern of threats differs across groups.

Source: Ward et al. (2021) , A national-scale dataset for threats impacting Australia's imperilled flora and fauna. Data reflect earlier threat assessments, including EPBC status information from 2018. Only fauna were included. Percentages use the number of assessed taxa within each animal group.

FIGURE 09 / OVERLAPPING THREATS

Wildlife rarely faces just one threat

Habitat loss, invasive species, fire and other pressures often affect the same animal. This chart shows the most common combinations of five selected threats recorded for Australia's imperilled fauna.

Threat recorded Threat not recorded

Hover over the bars or dots for the exact combination and number of taxa. Threat categories not shown here can still affect these animals.

What stands out: The most frequent profile includes habitat loss, invasive species/disease and adverse fire regimes together (64 taxa). These results show why protecting wildlife often requires addressing several environmental pressures at once.

Source: Ward et al. (2021), A national-scale dataset for threats impacting Australia's imperilled flora and fauna (supplementary species-threat matrix). Based on 456 unique assessed fauna taxa. A combination is exact only among these five categories and does not exclude additional threats outside them. Each taxon is counted once.
